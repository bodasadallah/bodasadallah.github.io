<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33555" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PeerReveal: Learning to Generalize in AI Peer Review Detection</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Detects fully AI-generated versus AI-rewritten peer reviews across domains, with a new PeerShift benchmark; this is core AI-review detection work.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.33555v1/figure3_training_framework.png" alt="Main figure of PeerReveal: Learning to Generalize in AI Peer Review Detection" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 3: Adversarial generator-detector training. Frozen detector and quality feedback optimize Qwen3.5-9B through GRPO. Three-domain regular training yields PeerReveal-Base ; adding adversarial AI reviews and source-grounded rewrites yields PeerReveal .</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Can we distinguish fully AI-generated reviews from those using AI only to rewrite human judgement? Furthermore, can we do so reliably when reviews are generated from diverse AI models and workflows across scientific domains? To this end, we introduce PeerReveal, a new detector that grounds its decisions in learned relationships between a review's claims and reference claims drawn from AI-generated reviews of the same paper, complemented by review-level textual signals. We evaluate PeerReveal on PeerShift, a new, challenging test set of 5,430 reviews spanning multiple domains, including machine learning, life sciences, and social sciences, combining variation in disciplinary review conventions with diverse AI review-production mechanisms. With regular training, PeerReveal achieves 70.52% pooled Macro-F1 on this benchmark, improving over the strongest baseline by 11.48 points. We develop an effective and practical adversarial training framework that further improves the detector's generalization using a small local generator optimized to produce challenging reviews, raising pooled Macro-F1 from 70.52% to 82.40%. These results show that robust AI-review detection benefits from richer review-level evidence and adversarial training on more challenging generation processes.</p></details>

<div class="dn-paper">
<div class="dn-rank">#2 · Review Score Prediction · 5/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33175" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Feature-Based Explainability of Transformer Scoring Models: A Fidelity-First Framework for Automated Writing Assessment</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Explains transformer scoring models with surrogate feature regression and SHAP; the method could carry over to interpretable review score prediction.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.33175v1/figure1.png" alt="Main figure of Feature-Based Explainability of Transformer Scoring Models: A Fidelity-First Framework for Automated Writing Assessment" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Representative Prompt-Level Comparison of Feature Importance Across Traits</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Transformer-based automated writing scoring models can achieve strong agreement with human scores while providing limited transparency into the response characteristics associated with their predictions. This study evaluated a feature-based post-hoc explanation approach in which explanatory fidelity was established before feature importance was interpreted. English Language Arts responses from three writing prompts in Grades 6 and 8 were analyzed, with about 12,700 responses per prompt and separate DeBERTa-v3-base research models for Expression and Conventions. Each response was represented by 61 interpretable linguistic and writing-quality features. Four approaches - theory-driven composites, hierarchical feature groupings, exploratory factor analysis, and Random Forest surrogates - were compared in their ability to reproduce DeBERTa predictions. Random Forest surrogates produced the largest observed R2 values across all six prompt-by-trait conditions, with held-out R2 values from .81 to .95. SHAP analyses showed substantial overlap in high-importance features across traits, with 75% to 90% of the top 20 features shared. Trait differentiation occurred mainly through relative feature weighting: Expression showed greater importance for lexical and conceptual indicators, whereas Conventions showed greater importance for sentence-level and syntactic-control indicators. Under researcher-defined mappings, rubric correspondence was 90%-95% for Expression and 12%-18% for Conventions. These percentages are descriptive and conditional on the mappings and available linguistic features. The findings support a fidelity-first framework for examining apparent scoring priorities while distinguishing post-hoc evidence about model behavior from direct explanation of transformer internals.</p></details>
