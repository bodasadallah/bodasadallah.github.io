<div class="dn-rank">#1 · Citation Analysis · 7/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23546" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Language Models Make New Research Sound Old</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">LLMs overstate what related papers establish, a mismatch between claims and cited work; also predicts reviewers' novelty grades.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Researchers are increasingly using large language models (LLMs) to validate research questions. We find that LLMs make open research questions sound partly studied and overstate what related papers establish. Models recognize most of these overstatements when asked to check the papers' full texts separately. On 40 questions drawn from newly accepted papers, GPT-6-Astra correctly judges 98% to be unstudied, but states that status plainly in only 8% of ordinary answers. All six frontier systems show reporting gaps of 30-90 percentage points. Across 22 models given identical papers, higher capability is associated with more accurate judgments and larger reporting gaps. This reporting failure is asymmetric: models correctly identify studied questions in 88-100% of ordinary answers when we supply the answering papers. To correct this, we build a fixed answer from each model's judgment and separate checks of cited papers. This raises Astra's clear reporting of open status from 8% to 95%, with improvements in all six systems. Finally, we predict reviewers' novelty grades by combining several models' written ratings with a rule learned from graded examples, reaching the performance range reported by Think-Probe-Respond. Unlike TPR, our approach needs no access to model weights or internal activations, so it also works with closed-weight frontier models.</p></details>

<div class="dn-rank">#2 · Peer Review · 6/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24353" target="_blank" rel="noopener">When Figures Disagree with Text: Evaluating Autonomous Visual Evidence Verification in Scientific Papers</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Evaluates whether LLM reviewers autonomously find reviewer-documented figure-text inconsistencies in full manuscripts (PRISMM-Bench).</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.24353v1/figure1_teaser.png" alt="Main figure of When Figures Disagree with Text: Evaluating Autonomous Visual Evidence Verification in Scientific Papers" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1 : A figure–text contradiction that exists only between pixels and a caption clause, and where autonomous review stops. (a) Page 5 of 16 in an unmodified manuscript. The caption assigns textual input to the third setup, but the diagram routes a photograph into it. (b) The bar shows the 1,445</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Scientific figures carry evidence for the claims that cite them, so reviewing a paper means checking that a figure agrees with the text, and not only describing what the figure shows. Benchmarks of figure-text verification typically hand the model the figure, the claim, or the knowledge that an inconsistency exists, which leaves untested whether a model can find the figure worth checking in a complete paper, relate a visual element to the claim it conflicts with, and recognize the conflict. We study this autonomous setting on 31 full manuscripts, each carrying a reviewer-documented figure-text inconsistency from PRISMM-Bench that is hidden from the model. Six multimodal models review each PDF under a neutral prompt, and we then add one input at a time to the same manuscripts. Of 1,445 open-ended reviews, 60.6% mention the flawed figure, yet a grading rubric accepts only 3.3% as diagnosing its contradiction; a blind audit finds the rubric lenient, so we read these as rubric-accepted rates and compare conditions rather than absolute values. In a separate controlled run, naming the figure, without stating that it is flawed, raises rubric-accepted diagnosis from 4.3% to 20.4%, while a consistency instruction and coarse visual cues do not. When the contradiction is visible only in the rendered figure, text-only review yields no accepted diagnosis, while review of the rendered pages with the figure named reaches 15.8%. A baseline that lets the model choose which figures to check, ranked by centrality, stays at 4.8%. Deciding what to verify is thus a large part of the difficulty, and the selection rule we tested does not resolve it. Multimodal document understanding should be evaluated on whether a model can propose what to verify, not only answer questions about a figure it is handed.</p></details>

<div class="dn-rank">#3 · Citation Analysis · 6/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23530" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Dreaming Up Authors: Factual Prevalence and LLM Hallucinations in Academic Authorship Attribution</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">LLM hallucination of paper authorship tied to citation counts, relevant to fabricated or incorrect references in LLM citations.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Large Language Models (LLMs) are widely used for question answering but are prone to hallucinations, yet the underlying causes, particularly the role of factual prevalence in training data, remain underexplored. In this work, we study this through academic authorship attribution, hypothesizing that hallucinations are more likely for less prevalent papers. Using citation count as a proxy for factual prevalence, we curate a dataset of 1.92 million papers across eight scientific disciplines with stratified samples by citation level. Experiments on three state-of-the-art LLMs confirm our hypothesis, revealing a robust inverse relationship between hallucination rate and citation count across all models and disciplines, with hallucination rates exceeding 98% for rarely cited papers and falling below 37% for highly cited ones. Our in-depth analysis further shows that these failures primarily reflect retrieval limitations rather than knowledge absence, and hidden-state probing of open-source models shows that factual prevalence is encoded in pre-generation representations, providing convergent mechanistic evidence for the prevalence-hallucination relationship. The code and data are available at https://github.com/quinnzhong1/dreaming-up-authors.</p></details>
