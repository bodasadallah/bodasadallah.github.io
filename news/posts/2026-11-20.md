<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26286" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Twice-Told Critiques: Claim-Level Homogenization of Peer Review in the LLM Era</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Measures claim-level critique overlap across reviews of the same paper and links homogenization to LLM-flagged reviews at ICLR/NeurIPS/ICML.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Peer review relies on several readers finding different problems. We measure critique overlap in conference and journal reviews. At ICLR, measured overlap rose from 12.0% in 2026 to 21.3% in 2027, in reviews from the same stage of the review process, with year-on-year rises at NeurIPS, ICML and the F1000 and PLOS journals. A third ICLR review of a paper added 5.46 new problems to the other two in 2027, against 6.12 in 2026. We apply MELD, a detector of LLM-generated text, to all reviews. Within a paper, two reviews that MELD flags overlap more than two unflagged reviews: by 3.0 points at ICLR in 2024-2025, by 4.5 points in 2026-2027, and by 5.4 points across five journal sources. These results provide evidence that LLM-flagged reviews of one paper are less distinct from each other than unflagged reviews, and suggest that the diversity of critiques among reviews of a single paper is falling.</p></details>

<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Given a single text and a simple zero-shot prompt (no web access, tools, candidate lists, or writing samples), frontier models can answer "who wrote this?" at three increasingly specific levels: (1) whether an AI or human wrote it, (2) if an AI, which lab, and (3) if a human, often exactly who. The latest models (Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Carbon) match trained detectors like Pangram while also explaining their predictions in natural language, and Opus 5.5 names the correct lab for 98% of texts generated by models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google. Most strikingly, Fable 5.2 correctly names the author of 39% of public peer reviews published in Oct-Nov 2026 (78% on a subset of reviews from Nature), and identifies the username behind 69% of comments from active Redditors (with &gt;500 posts over the last year). Accuracy depends on exposure to an author's writing in pretraining data and on reasoning effort, and simply asking a model how familiar it is with someone predicts whether it can identify their writing. For anyone who has published enough writing online, frontier models present a greater threat to anonymity than commonly assumed, and defenses are limited.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25845" target="_blank" rel="noopener">LINEAGE: A Benchmark for Empirical Lineage Retrieval in AI Scientific Literature</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Extracts baseline-comparison relations from full papers, a functional citation type, building lineage graphs over AI literature.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25845v1/intro-fig.png" alt="Main figure of LINEAGE: A Benchmark for Empirical Lineage Retrieval in AI Scientific Literature" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1 : Illustration of empirical lineage relations. In Lineage , empirical baseline evidence induces directed lineage edges from baseline papers to source papers. Given a query paper, its ancestors and successors in the lineage graph define retrieval ground truth. These relations capture empiric</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Current research agents are moving beyond academic search toward literature synthesis and experiment planning. These workflows increasingly rely on literature retrieval systems, which serve as standalone systems or as components of research agents. However, existing scientific literature retrieval benchmarks mainly evaluate these methods using topical relevance or citation links as supervision, which cannot capture which prior methods are used for empirical comparison. To address this gap, we introduce Lineage, a benchmark for empirical lineage retrieval in AI scientific literature. An empirical lineage relation links a prior paper to a source paper that explicitly uses its method as an empirical comparison baseline. These relations form lineage graphs whose multi-hop neighborhoods provide retrieval ground truth for structural evaluation. To construct these graphs at scale, we develop Lineage-Constructor, which converts heterogeneous paper records and full-paper empirical baseline evidence into calibrated lineage graphs. Built from 121,048 papers across 17 top AI venues from 2018 to 2025, Lineage contains 3,950 graph components, 67,502 paper nodes, 164,948 lineage edges, and 80,000 multi-facet retrieval queries. Experiments with 19 representative retrieval systems show that empirical lineage retrieval remains challenging. Performance drops sharply with lineage distance, while structural metrics reveal that isolated retrieval hits often fail to recover closely connected empirical comparison neighborhoods.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26077" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Same Judgement, Different Scores: Numerical Scoring Topology in LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">How score-scale design shifts LLM judge scores and calibration; relevant to LLM score prediction setups.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.26077v1/Numerical-Scoring-Topology-Overview.png" alt="Main figure of Same Judgement, Different Scores: Numerical Scoring Topology in LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Overview of the scoring-topology sensitivity analysis. We keep the evaluation prompt, model output, evaluation criteria, and judge model fixed while varying only the numerical scoring topology. Scores are normalized and compared across representative benchmarks.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>LLMs are increasingly used as automated evaluators to reduce the cost and time required for human annotation. However, the role of scoring-scheme design in the reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation remains underexplored. To address this problem, we introduce numerical scoring topology, which characterizes the structural design of a scoring scheme in terms of its scale width, granularity, offset, symmetry, and boundary placement. Building on this definition, we conduct the first systematic study of how numerical scoring topology influences zero-shot LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation across diverse benchmarks and judge models. Our results show that numerical scoring topology systematically affects judge scores, calibration, and robustness, particularly in judgment-intensive evaluations. Shifted and narrow ranges tend to induce some of the largest score deviations across many settings. We further find that stronger and larger judge models generally achieve higher discriminative validity and greater robustness to scoring-topology changes. These findings suggest that numerical scoring topology should be treated as an integral component of LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation and highlight the need for more principled and standardized scoring protocols.</p></details>
