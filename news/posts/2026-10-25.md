<div class="dn-rank">#1 · Citation Analysis · 9/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30355" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CiteAnchor: Source-Aware Grounding for Hallucinated Citation Detection and Resolution</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Benchmark and framework for detecting and resolving hallucinated citations, which is central to the LLM-citation fabrication strand.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30355v1/CiteANCHOR_main_figure_v5.png" alt="Main figure of CiteAnchor: Source-Aware Grounding for Hallucinated Citation Detection and Resolution" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Overview of CiteAnchor with a running example. CiteAnchor retrieves source-specific evidence, verifies citation fields against the retrieved records, and resolves the underlying source when one work is uniquely supported. Otherwise, it abstains from source resolution.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Hallucinated citations make it harder to trace sources and verify the evidence behind scientific claims. Detecting them is difficult because bibliographic records can be incomplete or differ across source types and publication versions. A plausible match alone neither validates the citation nor establishes its underlying source. We introduce CiteResolve, a benchmark of 423 real-world hallucinated citations annotated for source recoverability. We further propose CiteAnchor, a source-aware grounding framework that adapts retrieval and field-level verification to each source type and separately determines citation validity and whether a unique source can be recovered. On an existing detection benchmark, CiteAnchor achieves 0.841 F1 with GPT-5.6 Luna and 0.784 with an open-weight backbone, outperforming the baseline with the same backbones by 7.9 and 36.8 points. On CiteResolve, it achieves 74.7% and 70.7% end-to-end task success, outperforming the corresponding web search baselines by 16.3 and 65.3 percentage points. Our code and data are available at https://github.com/oldwizard7/CiteANCHOR_code.</p></details>

<div class="dn-rank">#2 · Peer Review · 7/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31414" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Major Revision, Minor Accountability: LLMs as Reviewers of Published Articles in Top-Tier Journals</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">LLM-generated reviews of published papers show harsh, inconsistent recommendations and low detection of planted flaws, which bears on AI reviewer evaluation.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>With the growth of scientific output, the peer review system is approaching a breaking point: reviewers are increasingly hard to secure, and turnaround times lengthen accordingly. LLM-assisted or LLM-generated peer review has been proposed as a remedy, but how such models would treat manuscripts already published in top-tier journals, the closest thing the current system has to a gold standard of scientific validity, is not known. Here we assembled a corpus of 200 articles, each released under a CC BY 4.0 license and published in 2026, from 20 general-science, life-science, and clinical-medicine journals including Nature, Science, and Cell, and asked four current LLMs (two proprietary, two open-weight) to produce independent peer-review reports of each one under a blinded protocol. Their decisions were overwhelmingly harsh and starkly inconsistent with each other: 605 of 800 reviews (75.6%) recommended major revision or rejection, but the rate at which any single model did so ranged from 17.5% to 100% (p &lt; 0.001), disagreement on manuscripts that had, by construction, already survived real editorial review at the journals in question. When the same manuscripts were doctored with one deliberately planted, field-specific methodological or statistical flaw, detection rates varied from 17.0% to 60.5% across models (p &lt; 0.001). Recommending revision for papers that had already passed editorial review (low specificity) while missing flaws deliberately placed in front of them (low sensitivity), these models produce output that should be read for what it is: an uneven, framing- and model-dependent signal.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31317" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SurveyGraph: Generating Academic Surveys via Methods-Entity Knowledge Graphs</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Survey generation over a method-relation knowledge graph, with audits of citation-grounding support in generated sentences.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31317v1/pipeline_new.png" alt="Main figure of SurveyGraph: Generating Academic Surveys via Methods-Entity Knowledge Graphs" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: The SurveyGraph pipeline. Starting from a user-specified topic, the system constructs a Methods-Entity Knowledge Graph through multi-source collection and four-layer relation inference, then uses the graph for core-paper selection, hybrid outline generation, graph-aware retrieval, and evol</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Most automated survey generation systems retrieve papers as flat documents, limiting their ability to capture research structure. We present SurveyGraph, a framework built around a Methods-Entity Knowledge Graph (MEKG) encoding method-level follow-on, competition, and shared-resource relations. The graph supports core-paper discovery, retrieval, hybrid outlining, and evidence-gated narrative synthesis. We evaluate retrieval, semantic edges, citation grounding, collection coverage, and generation-stage components. In a controlled seven-method equal-budget benchmark, MEKG-anchored fusion balances recall and structural density, with an independent co-citation check. In a separate archived graph-native diagnostic, graph-aware retrieval nearly doubles recall over flat lexical baselines across eight runs (p = 0.004). A semantic evidence gate achieves AUC = 0.75. Audits of 160 generated sentences find 80.6% anchored-or-partial citation support. Human adjudication of 108 stratified semantic edges finds 68.5% supported-or-partial relations (binary-support Cohen's kappa = 0.64), with improves strongest (80.6%) and extends weakest (52.8%). A SurveyBench-facing collection probe reaches mean reference coverage of 0.521 across four topics with an eight-paper budget. Same-backend Qwen diagnostics on a 30-paper/74-relation graph show reduced organization signals when hybrid outlining is removed. Concurrent-system comparisons provide bounded context rather than matched-backend end-to-end evidence. The results position MEKG as an auditable structural layer for typed diagnostics, relation-specific filtering, and controlled survey generation. Code and artifacts: https://github.com/xzzvsxd/SurveyGraph.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30549" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AI-Native Academia Should Build a Community Verification Layer</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Position paper on community verification infrastructure as an alternative to post-publication review.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This paper argues that AI-native academia should build a Community Verification Layer: interoperable, policy-governed infrastructure that converts researchers' routine reading and checking into persistent, claim- and version-linked evidence, while routing scarce deeper verification toward uncertain, consequential, and neglected research. AI is lowering the cost of producing plausible research artifacts faster than the cost of accountable evaluation, yet much of the evaluation researchers already perform while reading, citing, implementing, or extending work is never made reusable. We propose a tiered ladder from comprehension notes to claim checks, audits, artifact execution, reproduction, replication, and downstream validation. The layer would elicit judgments before revealing social signals, preserve multidimensional disagreement, privately verify contributors while allowing flexible public disclosure, allocate attention under expertise and coverage constraints, and make verification labor citable, with credit tied to the quality and depth of the evidence. We distinguish this design from prior open and post-publication review and outline governance requirements and potential counterarguments.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30987" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Interpretive Variation and Discourse Structure in Detecting Enthymemes in Controversial Political Discourse</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Argument mining of implicit premises with annotator-disagreement-aware evaluation and the role of discourse structure.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30987v1/figures/learning_curve.png" alt="Main figure of Interpretive Variation and Discourse Structure in Detecting Enthymemes in Controversial Political Discourse" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Out-of-fold (OOF) macro-F1 as a function of training-pool size (honest cross-validation, per-fold tuned thresholds). The arrow next to the metric name gives the direction of improvement ( ↑ \uparrow : higher is better). Shaded bands give ± \pm 1 sd over repeated draws; the full-size points</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Enthymemes are arguments that leave a premise or conclusion unstated, and they are a common persuasion device on social media. Because readers must fill in the missing part themselves, they often disagree on whether a text contains one. This paper asks how to detect and evaluate enthymemes when such disagreement is part of the task. On tweets about vaccination and immigration, each labeled by five annotators, F1 stalls around 0.67 for every fine-tuned model we train, regardless of data size, added features or label type. We therefore ask how these models should be evaluated instead, and propose metrics that compare predictions with the full distribution of annotator votes, together with a model that predicts this distribution directly and captures disagreement better at no cost in F1. We then ask what role discourse structure plays. Models learn to rely on explicit argumentative structure: they reliably recognize tweets without an enthymeme but rarely commit on tweets that contain one. Both the models and a discourse parser miss arguments whose links are left implicit and must be inferred by the reader. Finally, we discuss what these blind spots mean for content moderation systems trained on majority labels.</p></details>
