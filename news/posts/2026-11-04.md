<div class="dn-lede">The day is dominated by "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming," a paper-like blog post by Takehi, Teoh, Zhang and Khattab. It claims that frontier LLMs, GPT-6 Astra above all, can read raw neural-network weights to tell what a small network was trained to do, and can edit Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights to improve its math without gradients. A widely shared critique says the post is misleading. <h2 class="dn-section">Big news</h2>

<div class="dn-item">
<h3><a href="https://latent.space/p/ainews-llms-can-read-write-neural-network-weights-weight-space-programming-cuts-autoresearch-out-of/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">[AINews] LLMs can read &amp; write neural network weights: "Weight Space Programming" cuts Autoresearch out of the RSI loop</a></h3>
<div class="dn-meta">Latent Space · Nov 03</div>
<p>A blog post by Takehi, Teoh, Zhang and Khattab claims GPT-6 Astra can tell what a small network does from its raw weights, write game-playing nets that beat PPO-trained ones, and change 0.016% of Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights to improve its math up to 6x without gradients. The claim is disputed: a much-discussed critique by honnibal says the weight-reading and Qwen-fixing experiments use completely different techniques. Simon Willison reports that Qwen 3.8 27B on his laptop can reproduce one of the weight-interpretation examples.</p>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02244" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Can AI Reviewers See Your Efforts to Improve Your Paper? A Large-Scale Study of Paper Revision Trajectories</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Tests whether AI reviewer scores track manuscript revisions and user framing, which bears on auditing score changes.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.02244v1/papertrace-overview.png" alt="Main figure of Can AI Reviewers See Your Efforts to Improve Your Paper? A Large-Scale Study of Paper Revision Trajectories" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: PaperTrajectory overview. We collect arXiv version histories for papers accepted at five AI conferences in 2026. We select paper versions with a time gap of more than 30 days and pair consecutive selected versions. GPT-6 Astra characterizes each pair across 14 revision dimensions and judge</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>AI reviewers are increasingly used to assess scientific manuscripts, yet it remains unclear whether their judgments reflect the improvements authors make through revision. We investigate this question using PaperTrajectory-2026, the 2026 release of PaperTrajectory, a dataset of 6,959 paper trajectories across five AI conferences, with automatic annotations of changes along 14 dimensions and separate judgments of overall quality. The annotations show that major improvements emphasize empirical evidence and framing, while later revisions tend toward incremental improvement. Using independent assessments of manuscript versions from eight AI reviewers, we examine whether annotated major improvements receive higher scores, which revision dimensions are associated with score changes, and whether user opinions can outweigh observed revision gains. Every reviewer leaves scores unchanged on most major improvements, and agreement on the direction of score changes is weak. Revision dimensions show some reviewer-specific associations with score increases, but provide limited predictive discrimination and no consistent guide across reviewers. Even when a revision earns a higher neutral score, a brief negative user opinion can erase or reverse that advantage, with substantial variation across reviewers. These findings show that substantive revisions are not consistently reflected in review scores and that observed scoring gains can be vulnerable to user framing, motivating assessments that connect manuscript evidence to overall judgments and remain stable when no scientific evidence changes.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01313" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Are Fake Citations a Form of Reward Hacking? A Pre-Registered Study of Post-Training and Citation Cues</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Pre-registered study of why post-trained LLMs fabricate citations, which bears directly on hallucinated references in LLM writing.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Post-trained language models fabricate citations at high rates. We ask whether that is reward hacking: scoring well on a proxy for scholarship while doing worse on what it stands for. We ran four pre-registered experiments on 900 frozen questions (US federal case law, computationally adjacent scientific literature, and medical literature) across twelve open-weights checkpoints covering four post-training stages of three model families, then refit on fifteen. The clearest result holds the weights fixed and changes only the prompt. Asking for citations raises fabrication sharply in post-trained checkpoints and not in base ones: a 47.8-point interaction on twelve checkpoints (Holm-adjusted p = 2e-34) and 41.5 points on fifteen, in all three domains. A length-matched control separates the citation cue from a demand for a longer answer, so the effect is specific to citations, not verbosity. Post-training also raises fabrication overall, from 4.8% to 56.7%, but our registered prediction that it would rise most where fabrication is most tempting did not hold. A preference model trained only on surface citation features prefers post-trained output 86% of the time, and holding citation count fixed shows it rewards quantity. Our answer: possibly, but not proven. Post-trained models produce fabricated citations at far higher rates when asked for sources, and a naive evaluator that counts sources without checking them would prefer their output for citing more. But the targeted pattern we registered did not appear, and plain instruction-following could explain the same results. Proving that the reward causes fabrication needs an experiment that changes the reward during training, which this study does not do. Pre-registration: doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/S53HY. Code and data: https://github.com/zainnab-sparq/citation-reward-hacking</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03060" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SAGE (Subjectivity-Aware Generation Estimator): A Linguistic Framework for AI-Generated Review Detection *</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Detects AI-generated peer reviews using subjectivity and speech-act features, which resemble interpretable review-text features.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>We propose a simple, linguistically motivated approach to detecting AI-generated peer reviews based on the inherently human trait of subjectivity. We capture subjectivity at two levels: the semantic level of evaluative language and modality and the pragmatic level of speech acts. Using features derived from speech act annotations and model-predicted subjectivity scores, we train a lightweight classifier that achieves an AUROC of 0.9566 with only these linguistic features. Combining its output with the state-of-the-art detector Sem-Detect raises the TPR at 1% FPR of Sem-Detect on the APRDB benchmark from 0.874 to 0.985, outperforming the current state of the art. Our results show that linguistic features of subjectivity provide an explainable source of information for detecting AI-generated peer reviews and can be readily integrated into existing detection methods. The code and models are publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/Arys02/generated_ text_detection_hub) and Hugging Face (https://huggingface.co/Arys02), respectively.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03424" target="_blank" rel="noopener">IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Compares citations in LLM-written research proposals with what human communities actually cited.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03424v1/introduction.png" alt="Main figure of IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Overview of IdeaTaste. The literature corpus is frozen through 2025. In the upper branch, the human research community goes on to publish 2026 papers that cite work in the corpus. In the lower branch, a population of LLM agents retrieves from the corpus and writes research proposals. Match</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Research taste, the judgment of what to read, which problems to pursue, and which methods to try, increasingly shapes the work of AI scientists, yet it remains far less understood than their ability to execute research. We introduce IdeaTaste, a benchmark that scores an LLM by how closely the research proposals it writes from a literature snapshot frozen at 2025 match the work the same communities published in 2026. Our experiments span six contemporary language models and four research areas from established to emerging fields, covering more than 90{,}000 proposals in total. LLM agents fall well short of human communities in both the literature they cite and the ideas they state, reaching about 26% of the human level on citation matching and only 18% on idea matching. Their populations can cite as diversely as a community and take up its tasks and methods, yet rarely arrive at the concrete approaches and research plans it pursues. Neither richer field context nor larger populations substantially close this gap. In blinded expert review, AI proposals trail the 2026 papers least in presentation and most in expected success, echoing their gap in concrete approaches, and those whose ideas no human paper shares fare worst, suggesting weaker directions rather than overlooked opportunities. These results indicate that the main bottleneck in AI research taste lies less in finding the directions a community works on than in turning them into the concrete approaches it goes on to pursue. We release IdeaTaste as an open, continuously updated leaderboard (https://cxcscmu.github.io/IdeaTaste) that benchmarks contemporary AI proposals against future publication cycles.</p></details>

<div class="dn-rank">#5 · Peer Review · 5/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01076" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Change the Funnel, Not Who May Submit: Engineering Peer Review for the Flood</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Simulates conference review processes and review policies, calibrated to the NeurIPS consistency experiments.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Every reviewer knows the feeling: more papers each cycle, three full reviews for each of them, and a suspicion that near the accept line the outcome is luck. The numbers bear it out. NeurIPS received 30,709 submissions in 2026, more than three times as many as in 2021, and reviewing them took some 184,000 expert hours, or 92 years of researchers' time. Three-quarters of those hours went to papers that were rejected. And when NeurIPS twice had the same papers reviewed by two committees, the second rejected half of what the first accepted. Conferences are responding by limiting who may submit. We simulated that answer, and an alternative, in a model calibrated to the NeurIPS experiments. The limits save little and select no better, and those based on a past acceptance fall mostly on new authors. The alternative changes how papers are judged, spending full reads only where a decision turns on them. In a staged funnel, an automated quality check first turns back papers that are not ready. The rest are ranked by their value, from short summaries, by readers that each submission brings. Only the finalists get a full read, which checks their claims and settles the final ranking. The staged funnel needs a little over half of today's expert hours, accepts 95.5% of papers in the top 3-10% (against 74% today), and makes nearly every top-0.5% paper a spotlight or oral. And because every paper brings its own reader, the funnel keeps pace with the flood.</p></details>
