<h2 class="dn-section">Big news</h2>

<div class="dn-item">
<h3><a href="https://honnibal.dev/blog/astra-read-write-misleading" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The 'Astra can read and write weights to improve Qwen' post is misleading</a></h3>
<div class="dn-meta">Hacker News · Nov 03 · 1027 points, 207 comments</div>
<p>A critique on honnibal.dev with more than 1,000 Hacker News points argues that 'Sparks of Weight-Space Programming' (Takehi, Teoh, Zhang, Khattab) is misleading. The paper claims GPT-6 Astra can tell what a network was trained to do from its raw weights and can improve Qwen without gradients, but the critique says the weight-reading and Qwen-fixing experiments use completely different techniques. Latent Space says some claims were presented too favourably but that several groups have replicated parts independently, and Simon Willison reports that Qwen 3.8 27B on his laptop can interpret weights too. It's a useful case study in how claims in a 'paper-shaped' blog post get checked in public rather than through peer review.</p>
</div>

<h3><a href="https://bsky.app/profile/ehudreiter.bsky.social/post/3mzi7yz2ezs2t" target="_blank" rel="noopener">TACL update: Papers which were submitted to ARR or xACL conferences within the past 9 months cannot be submitted to TACL. This includes…</a></h3>
<div class="dn-meta">Ehud Reiter on Bluesky · Nov 04 · 6 likes, 3 reposts</div>
<p>TACL will no longer accept papers that were submitted to ARR or any *ACL conference in the past nine months. This includes papers that lost the ARR reviewer-capacity lottery or were desk-rejected by ARR. The policy changes how papers can move between ACL venues, and it confirms that ARR now turns papers away when it lacks reviewers.</p>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06090" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Overview of the NTCIR-19 SciClaimEval Task: Cross-modal Claim Verification in Scientific Papers</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Shared-task overview of SciClaimEval, an anchor paper's dataset, on verifying scientific claims against tables and figures in papers.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06090v1/example_1.png" alt="Main figure of Overview of the NTCIR-19 SciClaimEval Task: Cross-modal Claim Verification in Scientific Papers" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 2 . <details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This paper presents an overview of \emph{SciClaimEval}, a pilot task at NTCIR-19 on verifying scientific claims against the tables and figures of the papers in which they appear. Our dataset pairs authentic claims from 180 papers in machine learning, natural language processing, and medicine with contrastive versions of their tables and figures, one supporting the claim and one minimally modified so that it does not. The task comprises two subtasks: deciding whether a piece of evidence supports a claim, and selecting which of two evidence versions does. Twenty-two teams registered, the highest number among NTCIR-19 tasks, and 14 submitted 177 runs. Most systems built on multimodal large language models, relying on prompting and multi-stage inference rather than task-specific fine-tuning. On the first subtask, the best systems reached 98.1% pair accuracy, but all six top-ranked systems relied on the contrastive pairs; the best system that judged each claim-evidence pair on its own scored 24.3 points lower. Open-weight models, embedded in carefully designed pipelines, matched proprietary ones. Yet verifying a single claim against its evidence, the setting that matters in practice, remains an open problem.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06789" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ranking Agreement Does Not Reveal Paragraph-Edit Agreement in Two Released Paper Acceptance Predictors</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Audits released ICLR acceptance predictors and finds their ranking agreement does not carry over to judging which paragraph edits raise scores.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06789v1/fig2_edit_fidelity.png" alt="Main figure of Ranking Agreement Does Not Reveal Paragraph-Edit Agreement in Two Released Paper Acceptance Predictors" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 2: Surrogate changes barely track target changes for the same edits. (a, b) Density of all 3,323 admitted single edits across 199 papers: surrogate change against the change for T 1 T_{1} (a) and T 2 T_{2} (b) in the post hoc 32-bit rescoring of the released weights, each in units of its eval</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>A fast acceptance classifier could select revisions for a slower evaluator. We ask whether agreement on existing papers supports that use. We compare a classifier of word and document features with two released text-only acceptance predictors fine-tuned from the same language model with three billion parameters. For 198 papers from the International Conference on Learning Representations, we compare model rankings of the original papers and of paragraph rewrites screened automatically for unchanged facts, claim strength, and attribution. The classifier ranks original papers closely with one predictor and moderately with the other, but shows no detectable agreement with either on which rewrites raise scores. The tests specified in advance used reduced numerical precision. Rounding error dominated the small score responses. We therefore rescored the released weights in full 32-bit precision as a post hoc check; edit agreement remained near zero. At this precision, when each predictor chooses among five rewrites per paper, its average raw score is under two points out of 100 higher than with random choice. The evidence covers presentation rewrites and two open predictors from one family; transfer to costly evaluators remains untested. Revision selectors should therefore be checked against the intended evaluator on the revisions they rank.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05459" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Can Large Language Models Review the Scientific Literature? A Test Against a 102-Article Benchmark</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">LLM literature-review syntheses contain unsupported claims and quotation errors, and tools misjudge passing mentions as substantial discussion.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.05459v1/figures/workflow_inputs_tasks_evaluation.png" alt="Main figure of Can Large Language Models Review the Scientific Literature? A Test Against a 102-Article Benchmark" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Workflow of the study, showing the inputs, four tasks, and their evaluation. The closed corpus contains the same 102 source-article PDFs for interpretation, synthesis, and research-gap proposals. The benchmark review and its classifications were withheld from the tools and used for evaluat</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to support scientific literature reviews, yet evaluations often examine individual tasks rather than the review process as a whole. We assess six AI tools across four stages: finding relevant articles, interpreting them, synthesizing across them, and proposing future research. The benchmark is a published review of the Spanish plume weather pattern and its 102-article corpus. In the initial searches, five tools collectively identified 57 articles, with the best individual tool finding 42. Institutional library access increased discovery from 42 to 82 articles for Codex and from 15 to 79 for Claude Code. Given the complete corpus of 102 PDFs, three tools correctly identified all 18 articles discussing the Spanish plume substantially, but incorrectly classified 40% to 79% of the 84 passing mentions as substantial. The same three tools produced syntheses evaluated blindly by two domain experts. Although largely accurate, the syntheses provided limited insight and sometimes contained unsupported claims, quotation errors, and exaggerated statements of consensus. Finally, all three tools proposed research directions broader than the Spanish plume literature, although two approached questions identified in the benchmark review. No single tool performed best across all tasks. We provide eight recommendations for researchers using AI tools in literature reviews. Our findings suggest that LLMs can assist with discovering and interpreting scientific literature, particularly when institutional access is available, but cannot replace expert judgment in evaluating evidence or synthesizing research.</p></details>
