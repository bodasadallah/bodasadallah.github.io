<div class="dn-meta">Microsoft Research · Nov 10</div>
<p>Microsoft researchers describe 'Lazarus', a technique that reprograms dormant neurons to plant a backdoor in an agent model without retraining. <div class="dn-paper">
<div class="dn-rank">#1 · Peer Review · 7/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12820" target="_blank" rel="noopener">How Well Does AI Peer Review Work? A Benchmark Built from 100 Planted Errors</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Benchmarks AI reviewers' ability to catch errors planted in papers, a direct evaluation of AI reviewers.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>One hundred known errors were planted into 10 open-access psychology papers, and the modified papers were then run through frontier models and two commercial AI review tools. The best single system caught 71 of the 100 errors, while the worst caught 30. Pooling every system's output caught 93. Models are only partly correlated in the errors they find, which makes ensembling a big lever for finding issues in papers. Seven errors could not be caught by any system, and all seven were omissions: information deleted from a paper rather than mistakes inserted into it. Refine.ink contributes more unique catches than any other single system, though it is expensive on a per-error basis. False positives were not measured, and how this error distribution compares to the distribution of errors in real papers remains unknown. The papers, errors, model outputs, and full experiment log are public, so that others can build on this toward a comprehensive evaluation benchmark across disciplines.</p></details>

<div class="dn-paper">
<div class="dn-rank">#2 · NLP · 6/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11174" target="_blank" rel="noopener">What Changed in Engineering Writing After Generative AI Chat Assistants: Evidence from 1.8 Million Articles</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Large-scale study of LLM-induced lexical and hedging shifts in scientific writing, which bears on human versus LLM scholarly text.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.11174v1/fig_excess_vocabulary.png" alt="Main figure of What Changed in Engineering Writing After Generative AI Chat Assistants: Evidence from 1.8 Million Articles" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 2 : Excess Ratio of Every Word Occurring at Least 400 Times, with Marker and Control Words Labeled.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This study asks what changed in engineering articles after conversational language models became publicly available on November 30, 2022, and develops methods for measuring such change in a field without a uniform abstract index. It assembled 1,839,962 articles published from 2015 to 2025 in 256 engineering journals, tracked twelve measures of wording, style, and article structure, and compared each with the projection of its own pre-2023 trend. Building the monthly series showed that records dated only by year fall on January 1 and create false January peaks unless removed. In abstracts, 31 words associated with language model output appeared 82% more often than projected (55.4 against 27.0 per 10,000 words in 2025), while four control words of the same formal register fell 16%. Words that soften a claim, such as may, fell 13%, words that strengthen one, such as clearly, rose 20%, and long words rose 11%. Author counts, reference lists, page counts, and publication volume stayed within 4% of trend. The change appears in all fourteen subject categories, and false release dates, dropping any one journal or word, and word lists chosen from the data support it. A random sample of 12,866 full texts from the same corpus, including subscription articles and weighted back to it, shows the same words rising 99% in article bodies. Statements disclosing AI writing assistance, measured apart from mentions of language models, went from none to 3.7% in 2025, four in five of them at one publisher. The design is descriptive, not causal.</p></details>
