<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30410" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Disclosure Bias in AI Reviewers</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Controlled study of how AI-use disclosure statements shift AI reviewer ratings and acceptance on ICLR submissions; relevant to review-score auditing.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>AI models increasingly help to review papers, and many conferences and journals now require authors to state how they used AI. Does this AI-use statement change how an AI reviewer rates a paper, and could it change which papers are accepted? In a preliminary study, we varied only the AI-use statement of 150 ICLR 2027 submissions, in up to nine versions adapted from real statements: from no AI, through writing help and coding, to an AI research agent and an AI author. Six AI reviewers rated the versions: the decision model Jev and the language models Claude Haiku 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemini 4 Argon. Across 23,652 reviews, we find that every reviewer rates papers lower on average when the statement says AI did all the work than under a statement of no AI use. The AI research agent statement lowers the rating significantly for Jev, Sonnet and Argon. We find no evidence of a disclosure bias for AI assistance: for writing help, coding and research help, no reviewer's rating differs significantly from its rating under a statement of no AI use. Whether the observed penalty changes which papers are accepted depends on more than the size of the penalty: on how finely and how consistently the reviewer separates the papers near the acceptance boundary, on whether the penalty is spread over all papers or concentrated on a few, and possibly on how many other papers disclose AI use.</p></details>
