<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17834" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PaperCritic: Training AI Reviewers to Assess the Soundness of Papers</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Quality-labeled ICLR review-point dataset on soundness and RL-trained AI reviewer; matches review-point decomposition work.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17834v1/Images/scored_quality_by_source_stacked_v2.png" alt="Main figure of PaperCritic: Training AI Reviewers to Assess the Soundness of Papers" loading="lazy"><figcaption>(c)</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>As AI conference submissions grow rapidly, AI reviewers are being considered as an alternative. However, simply imitating human reviewing is problematic because reviewing serves multiple purposes, including evaluating papers' soundness, interestingness, and clarity, the last two of which are subjective. In this work, we focus solely on evaluating soundness, the most important and objective criterion, and define the learning objective as jointly maximizing (1) precision, based on the soundness and substantiveness of review points, and (2) recall, the coverage of high-quality reference review points. Under this problem formulation, we construct a quality-labeled review point dataset, PaperSound-23K, using 1K ICLR submissions and 23K human-written and LLM-generated review points. Using this dataset, we train an AI reviewer model, PaperCritic, through reinforcement learning with the F1 score of precision and recall as the reward. Our experiments show that PaperCritic outperforms frontier models and models trained with other AI reviewer training methods on an in-domain test set. Surprisingly, learning to review ICLR submissions also transfers to reviewing Nature-family papers and even to out-of-domain reviewing tasks (e.g., code review, legal contract review, and feedback on story writing).</p></details>

<div class="dn-paper">
<div class="dn-rank">#2 · Peer Review · 8/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17811" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AI Reviews Are Biased Towards the Appendix</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Compares where LLM vs human reviewers draw weaknesses on ACL/ICLR papers; directly characterizes AI review behavior.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17811v1/review_method_diagram_2.png" alt="Main figure of AI Reviews Are Biased Towards the Appendix" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 2 : Pipeline of our method: i) Extract and sample papers from four conferences with a minimum appendix length of 15 pages, ii) Generate an AI review, and iii) map each weakness back to the section it belongs to.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used by reviewers to highlight weaknesses during the reviewing process, and their outputs often shape the reviews that humans submit. Prior work studies how LLM reviews align with human ones, yet we know little about which parts of a paper these models actually criticize. In this work, we investigate 2,000 papers from ACL, EMNLP, COLM, and ICLR, and show that the latest GPT and Claude models draw about 50% or more of their review weaknesses from the appendix. This behavior holds across the API, coding-agent, and web access methods for using LLMs to review a paper. In contrast, we find that humans draw only 8.4% of their weaknesses from the appendix, and these weaknesses primarily revolve around a paper's presentation and content related to its core idea. Meanwhile, LLMs draw weaknesses from deep in the appendix that concern fine-grained technical details not central to the paper's contributions. We anticipate that our analysis will contribute to the scholarly discourse on AI reviews. Our code is publicly available at: https://github.com/cincynlp/review_appendix.</p></details>
