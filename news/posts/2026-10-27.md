<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.35098v1/Figures/Figure1_Research_Framework.jpg" alt="Main figure of Interpreting LLM Scores in Peer Review: Alignment and Divergence Across Prompts, Domains, and Manuscript Features" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Research Framework. Guided by an argument-based approach to validation, we compare four LLMs on 7,083 public ICLR 2024 submissions under a standard review condition and examine ten prompt conditions using DeepSeek-V3.2. We distinguish average score divergence, paper-level absolute error, and rank correlation, then examine domain differences and adjusted manuscript-feature associations. For DeepSeek-V3.2, Area Chair guidance reduces mean rating divergence from 1.460 to 0.199 points and mean absolute error from 1.577 to 0.942, while rank correlation changes from 0.377 to 0.387. Historical references combined with downward scoring instructions yield mean divergence of 0.052 and rank correlation of 0.321. Domain differences remain after global calibration. Longer titles have positive adjusted associations with rating divergence, and formula density has negative associations across all four configurations. These findings connect score interpretation to the scoring procedure, evaluation context, and manuscript information represented in the comparison. They show why aggregate alignment, manuscript ordering, and correspondence within manuscript groups require distinct evidence when evaluating LLM-generated scholarly assessment information.</p></details>

These models can operate at different levels of reasoning effort and provide summaries of their reasoning processes. <h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31791" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PaperScorer: How Predictable Is Paper Acceptance?</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Predicts ICLR accept/reject from paper text or images; useful baseline for acceptance prediction, and models fail on mid-scored borderline papers.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31791v1/teaser_flow.png" alt="Main figure of PaperScorer: How Predictable Is Paper Acceptance?" loading="lazy"><figcaption>(a) PaperScorer predicts a paper’s decision from its text or visual view.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Automated research is largely evaluated by benchmark performance, but the value of research rarely reduces to a single score. Humans judge a work's holistic contribution through peer review, which is hard to model because it is noisy and depends on many factors. However, what if, given only the paper, we could predict its peer-review decision directly? We introduce PaperScorer, a family of models that predict a paper's accept or reject decision from its extracted text or page screenshots, training only on the decision after removing author identity and formatting shortcuts. Vision models are the most predictive (68% accuracy on ICLR papers and 77% on ML papers from arXiv), beating text-only and frontier reasoning models. Additionally, its confidence is calibrated, so its most confident predictions are also its most accurate. <h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35793" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Quantifying Claim Smoothing by LLM Research Agents</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Measures whether claims in LLM research reports are stronger than the cited evidence supports, close to verifying cited support.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.35793v1/intro-image-smoothscore.png" alt="Main figure of Quantifying Claim Smoothing by LLM Research Agents" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: When synthesizing over many documents, agents can present a claim as well supported when the evidence is actually more mixed. <details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Deep research, or any use of large language models (LLMs) to read a large document collection and produce insights, requires synthesizing information from different sources. In this work, we find that LLMs produce long-form research reports that read as confident syntheses but which fail to accurately reflect the underlying documents. We call this failure smoothing. In collapsing diverse and potentially conflicting sources into a single narrative, the model discards the evidential "jaggedness" of its corpus and asserts conclusions more decisive than the citations can support. We introduce a claim level scoring pipeline, SmoothScore, to quantify this effect. Our pipeline extracts key conclusive claims from model generated reports, then finds evidence relevant to each claim and clusters that evidence along high level aspects. Finally, each claim is scored with respect to the evidence to quantify the degree of smoothing done by the agent. Across 7 systems and 4 datasets, we find that agents consistently produce stronger claims than warranted.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33018" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SUDO: From Scientific Publications to Global Scholarly Discourse</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">A discourse ontology for scholarly narratives that links papers into a global scholarly discourse graph.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.33018v1/figures/runing_example_v5.png" alt="Main figure of SUDO: From Scientific Publications to Global Scholarly Discourse" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1 : Illustration of how humans understand a publication’s meaning and its relationships to other scholarly works from its linguistic structure.</figcaption></figure>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32986" target="_blank" rel="noopener">LLM-as-a-Judge Version Shift: The Instrument Changed, Not the Standard</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Shows that changing the LLM judge version shifts leniency and score geometry when rating research proposals, relevant to LLM-as-judge methodology.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Automated research pipelines increasingly rely on fixed LLM judges to rank, filter, and revise proposals, yet these judges are routinely replaced by newer versions. We study whether such replacements preserve the underlying measurement standard. Using a preregistered, frozen-weight design, we evaluate three adjacent same-family transitions across Qwen, Gemma, and Mistral on frozen ML research proposals under randomized information conditions. The common assumption that newer judges are simply harsher fails: two transitions shift toward greater leniency, while the third shifts in the opposite direction on anchors but attenuates off-anchor. More importantly, version changes can alter not only score location but also criterion geometry and resolution; one transition collapses clean-flawed discrimination, placing 93% of pool scores on a single value. Supplying identical facts does not close the version gap, placebo-controlled interventions show that observed packet effects are largely format-driven rather than content-driven, and global recalibration repairs the mean while explaining little object-level dispersion. We organize these failures with a measurement model in which a judge replacement can change its origin, criterion geometry, or resolution. A small stratified anchor audit of about 200 calls identifies the failure class before deployment, and frozen thresholds correctly diagnose two post-registration API judge replacements. We release six preregistration locks and the full audit trail of 14882 judge calls.</p></details>

<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Research ideas are always proposed under resource constraints: a plan must fit the available compute, API credit, and time. Yet evaluations of research-ideation agents mostly score unconstrained novelty or overlap with a published paper, and their LLM judges are rarely checked against peer review. We introduce AlphaIdeaBench, a benchmark of 100 ideation tasks (50 workshop calls for papers and 50 open problems stated in surveys), each paired with an explicit resource budget and a shared proposal schema. <h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31810" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PaperFormalizer: Author-in-the-Loop Autoformalization of Research Papers in Lean</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Agentic auditing and Lean formalization of research-paper claims; related to paper consistency verification.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31810v1/ui_page.png" alt="Main figure of PaperFormalizer: Author-in-the-Loop Autoformalization of Research Papers in Lean" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 2: The page of the case-study paper (Section 5 ) after its run; the viewer carries the name of the public website, PaperFormalizer. The left column tracks the run’s stages (V1 is Stage 1, V1.5 Stage 2, F1–F1.5 Stage 3, F2 Stage 4, F2.5–F4 Stage 5, and F5–V6 Stage 6), gives the paper’s content</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Even a carefully proofread paper can leave small gaps in its proofs or its stated assumptions. We introduce PaperFormalizer, a Lean-based agentic pipeline that helps authors find and close such gaps in their own papers and turn each paper into a Lean-verified version. Starting from the LaTeX source, PaperFormalizer transcribes the paper into a dependency graph of statements, audits each statement in natural language for faithfulness, ambiguity and correctness, and then formalizes and proves the statements in Lean against Mathlib, optional domain libraries and the author's personal toolkit. Only the author knows what a statement is meant to say and how a formalization gap in a result is best fixed. The pipeline therefore stops and asks the author whenever a statement admits more than one reading, presents every repair, in natural language and TeX symbols, as a proposal that the author can accept, decline or redirect, and leaves to the author every decision that could potentially change what the paper claims. The output is an annotated and revised version of the paper, a report of the changes, and Lean code in which each declaration is linked to the statement it formalizes. The author's toolkit grows as more papers are formalized, and authors can share their verified papers so that researchers who build on them can reuse their Lean code. By making verified papers practical to produce, PaperFormalizer aims to make research that rests on mathematical proofs more robust.
