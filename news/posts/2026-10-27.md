<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34654" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Thinking-in-Bets at Test Time Reduces Sycophantic Revisions in LLM Agents</a></h3>
<div class="dn-meta">Hugging Face daily papers · Oct 25 · 0 upvotes</div>
<p>In this preprint, Thinking-in-Bets is a training-free method where an agent writes down its main claims, the checks behind them and its confidence before it reads user feedback, then consults that record when challenged. <h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35098" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Interpreting LLM Scores in Peer Review: Alignment and Divergence Across Prompts, Domains, and Manuscript Features</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">LLM rating alignment with human ICLR scores across prompts and manuscript features, a direct match for review score prediction and auditing.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.35098v1/Figures/Figure1_Research_Framework.jpg" alt="Main figure of Interpreting LLM Scores in Peer Review: Alignment and Divergence Across Prompts, Domains, and Manuscript Features" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Research Framework. Human scores are averaged across three to five reviewers and compared with each of four LLMs. The analysis covers score bias, absolute error, rank correlation, reviewer disagreement, and variation across ten DeepSeek prompt conditions, domains, and human-rating groups. </figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Large language model (LLM) scores are being studied as information for scholarly evaluation. Interpreting these scores requires evidence about agreement with human judgments and its variation across evaluation conditions. Guided by an argument-based approach to validation, we compare four LLMs on 7,083 public ICLR 2024 submissions under a standard review condition and examine ten prompt conditions using DeepSeek-V3.2. We distinguish average score divergence, paper-level absolute error, and rank correlation, then examine domain differences and adjusted manuscript-feature associations. For DeepSeek-V3.2, Area Chair guidance reduces mean rating divergence from 1.460 to 0.199 points and mean absolute error from 1.577 to 0.942, while rank correlation changes from 0.377 to 0.387. Historical references combined with downward scoring instructions yield mean divergence of 0.052 and rank correlation of 0.321. Domain differences remain after global calibration. Longer titles have positive adjusted associations with rating divergence, and formula density has negative associations across all four configurations. These findings connect score interpretation to the scoring procedure, evaluation context, and manuscript information represented in the comparison. They show why aggregate alignment, manuscript ordering, and correspondence within manuscript groups require distinct evidence when evaluating LLM-generated scholarly assessment information.</p></details>

<div class="dn-why">Studies how verifiable GPT-5.5's generated references are under different reasoning effort, which bears directly on fabricated LLM citations.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.34212v1/Fig1.png" alt="Main figure of Higher Reasoning Effort Improves Reference Verifiability and Changes Reasoning Summaries in GPT-5.5" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1 : Study design and analysis pipeline. <details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Large language models are increasingly being used in different stages of scientific knowledge production, including the suggestion of references. These models can operate at different levels of reasoning effort and provide summaries of their reasoning processes. This raises two questions: Does increasing reasoning effort improve the verifiability of generated references, and do the resulting reasoning summaries reveal how the model's behavior changes with greater effort? We examined summaries from GPT-5.5 as it generated scholarly references for 1,000 papers under paired low- and high-effort settings. Generated references were matched to external bibliographic records, and reasoning summaries were scored for four predefined features and explored for additional features discovered by GPT-5.5. High effort increased the mean paper-level resolver-verified reference rate from 78.3% to 84.8%, and produced longer reasoning summaries. All four predefined features received higher scores under high effort. The discovered features changed less uniformly: some appeared equally often in both conditions, whereas others were more common under high effort. Within each effort condition, neither feature set showed a consistent increase in verification rates with higher scores.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35254" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Style over Substance? Linguistic Bias in LLM-based Peer Review Copy title</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">LLM reviewers reward confident rhetoric and native-English style, a linguistic bias in automated peer review that relates to hedging and intensity features.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.35254v1/figure-LLM-reviewer-small-v2.png" alt="Main figure of Style over Substance? Linguistic Bias in LLM-based Peer Review Copy title" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Visualization of our controlled framework for linguistic rewriting of research papers.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>The number of paper submissions to major AI conferences has increased dramatically in recent years, substantially increasing the peer-review workload of researchers. At the same time, large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to support peer review, both by individual reviewers and by conference organizations. While LLM capabilities have improved considerably, their evaluations may be influenced by linguistic characteristics that are unrelated to scientific content. In this work, we introduce a controlled experimental framework for studying such effects by systematically perturbing the linguistic style of research papers. Specifically, we rewrite papers to exhibit (i) linguistic features associated with native or non-native English writing, and (ii) high- or low-confidence linguistic features. To construct these interventions, we derive fine-grained rewriting instructions grounded in the linguistic literature and apply them using LLMs. Crucially, our interventions are designed to preserve the underlying scientific content. We then evaluate the resulting papers using different LLM reviewers. Our experiments expose a critical vulnerability in LLM-based peer review: reviewers systematically reward confident rhetoric independent of scientific content, raising serious concerns about the robustness of their assessments.</p></details>

<div class="dn-rank">#4 · Citation Analysis · 7/10</div>
<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35793" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Quantifying Claim Smoothing by LLM Research Agents</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Measures whether claims in LLM research reports are stronger than the cited evidence supports, close to verifying cited support.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.35793v1/intro-image-smoothscore.png" alt="Main figure of Quantifying Claim Smoothing by LLM Research Agents" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: When synthesizing over many documents, agents can present a claim as well supported when the evidence is actually more mixed. SmoothScore flags these smoothed-over claims and quantifies the degree of smoothing.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Deep research, or any use of large language models (LLMs) to read a large document collection and produce insights, requires synthesizing information from different sources. In this work, we find that LLMs produce long-form research reports that read as confident syntheses but which fail to accurately reflect the underlying documents. We call this failure smoothing. In collapsing diverse and potentially conflicting sources into a single narrative, the model discards the evidential "jaggedness" of its corpus and asserts conclusions more decisive than the citations can support. We introduce a claim level scoring pipeline, SmoothScore, to quantify this effect. Our pipeline extracts key conclusive claims from model generated reports, then finds evidence relevant to each claim and clusters that evidence along high level aspects. Finally, each claim is scored with respect to the evidence to quantify the degree of smoothing done by the agent. Across 7 systems and 4 datasets, we find that agents consistently produce stronger claims than warranted.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33018" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SUDO: From Scientific Publications to Global Scholarly Discourse</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">A discourse ontology for scholarly narratives that links papers into a global scholarly discourse graph.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.33018v1/figures/runing_example_v5.png" alt="Main figure of SUDO: From Scientific Publications to Global Scholarly Discourse" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1 : Illustration of how humans understand a publication’s meaning and its relationships to other scholarly works from its linguistic structure.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Scholarly Knowledge Graphs (SKGs) support literature search, scientific discovery, and agent-assisted research. Yet, most approaches to SKG construction pre-specify the knowledge unit to be represented, typically named scholarly entities or contribution-level statements, and retain only the publication content expressible through that unit. This choice produces two dominant yet distinct paradigms: concept-centric SKGs that preserve entities and local relations, and statement-centric SKGs that capture high-level discourse in sentences. In both cases, the graph reflects a chosen extraction target rather than how a paper constructs and communicates knowledge. We introduce SUDO, the Scholarly Upper Discourse Ontology, which defines how knowledge elements are organized and related to form a paper's research narrative, namely its scholarly discourse. Using linguistic principles that generalize across fields and tasks, SUDO enables each publication's scholarly discourse to be constructed from the bottom up as a coherent, source-grounded representation. Applied consistently across papers, the same structure connects their research narratives into a global scholarly discourse without discarding local context or provenance. We demonstrate SUDO's capabilities across a comprehensive suite of scholarly knowledge-modeling use cases and performance improvements on a downstream Large Language Model (LLM)-based scientific QA task.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32986" target="_blank" rel="noopener">LLM-as-a-Judge Version Shift: The Instrument Changed, Not the Standard</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Shows that changing the LLM judge version shifts leniency and score geometry when rating research proposals, relevant to LLM-as-judge methodology.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Automated research pipelines increasingly rely on fixed LLM judges to rank, filter, and revise proposals, yet these judges are routinely replaced by newer versions. We study whether such replacements preserve the underlying measurement standard. Using a preregistered, frozen-weight design, we evaluate three adjacent same-family transitions across Qwen, Gemma, and Mistral on frozen ML research proposals under randomized information conditions. The common assumption that newer judges are simply harsher fails: two transitions shift toward greater leniency, while the third shifts in the opposite direction on anchors but attenuates off-anchor. More importantly, version changes can alter not only score location but also criterion geometry and resolution; one transition collapses clean-flawed discrimination, placing 93% of pool scores on a single value. Supplying identical facts does not close the version gap, placebo-controlled interventions show that observed packet effects are largely format-driven rather than content-driven, and global recalibration repairs the mean while explaining little object-level dispersion. We organize these failures with a measurement model in which a judge replacement can change its origin, criterion geometry, or resolution. A small stratified anchor audit of about 200 calls identifies the failure class before deployment, and frozen thresholds correctly diagnose two post-registration API judge replacements. We release six preregistration locks and the full audit trail of 14882 judge calls.</p></details>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33644" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Validates an LLM judge for idea novelty against reviewer-derived novelty scores from ICLR, NeurIPS and ICML reviews.</div>
<figure><img src="https://arxiv.org/html/2610.33644v1/Alphaframework.png" alt="Main figure of AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1: Overview of AlphaIdeaBench. Alignment Verification maps reviewer comments on 600 papers onto the four novelty axes before those axes are used on agent writes.</figcaption></figure>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Research ideas are always proposed under resource constraints: a plan must fit the available compute, API credit, and time. Yet evaluations of research-ideation agents mostly score unconstrained novelty or overlap with a published paper, and their LLM judges are rarely checked against peer review. We introduce AlphaIdeaBench, a benchmark of 100 ideation tasks (50 workshop calls for papers and 50 open problems stated in surveys), each paired with an explicit resource budget and a shared proposal schema. An LLM judge scores each proposal on 12 dimensions covering validity, novelty, and feasibility. To validate the judge, we compare its scores on four novelty axes (problem, method, scenario, and insight) with scores derived from reviewer comments on 600 papers from ICLR 2026, NeurIPS 2025, and ICML 2025. For problem and method novelty, Gemini 3.7 Flash is within one point of the reviewer-derived score on 94-97% of papers; agreement on scenario novelty is relatively lower. We evaluate eight published harnesses on a shared backbone and, within one harness, eight backbone LLMs. CoI-Agent obtains the highest overall score, narrowly ahead of SciMON, which leads on method and insight novelty. All harnesses score low on scenario novelty and rarely change the problem setting. The backbone largely determines which open problem a proposal targets, whereas the harness determines whether a proposal addresses one problem or combines several. Weaker backbones such as Llama 4 Scout fail to carry out that harness's strategy and score low on budget feasibility. Finally, when only the budget is varied on three topics, a larger budget changes the experimental setting on compute-intensive topics but only adds tests on an inexpensive one. We release the tasks, rubrics, and scores to foster standardized, reproducible benchmarking of future research-ideation agents under constraints.</p></details>
