<h3><a href="https://maxxbench.unlap.ai/research/why-maxxbench/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">MaxxBench – "Nice DeepSWE score. But is it true?"</a></h3>
<div class="dn-meta">Hacker News · Nov 07 · 395 points, 105 comments</div>
<p>MaxxBench claims that coding models with similar DeepSWE scores stop looking alike once the familiar tasks are changed slightly, for example by lowercasing a file name. Ethan Mollick also shared it. This is a write-up, not a peer-reviewed paper. It is a fresh case of benchmark scores not holding up under small changes, which matters for how you judge LLM evaluation results.</p>

<div class="dn-item">
<h3><a href="https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/9/details-summary/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Hide your slop PR descriptions behind</a></h3>
<div class="dn-meta">Simon Willison · Nov 09</div>
<p>Willison argues that agent-written PR descriptions are accurate and complete but still read like slop: readers skip them because they take longer to read than they took to write. His fix is to put a short human-written summary on top and fold the agent's text away below it. It is a practitioner's take on what makes LLM-written text easy to spot and off-putting, which bears on your work on human versus LLM writing.</p>
</div>

<h3><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08704" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Contextuality and Contradictions: Exploring Real-World Identifications of AI-Generated Writing</a></h3>
<div class="dn-why">Qualitative and computational study of how people identify AI-generated writing; adjacent to AI-text detection in scholarly writing and reviews.</div>
<details><summary>Abstract</summary><p>As AI-generated content spreads into nearly every domain of human interaction, practices and discourses of AI detection, both formal and informal, have become an everyday part of how people interact with text and with each other. In this paper, we explore the real-world practices and discourses of AI identification that have emerged in response to a shifting discursive landscape where the human provenance of a text can no longer be taken for granted. We pair qualitative coding with computational analysis of lexical change, grounded in linguistic anthropological perspectives on language and communication, to investigate AI identifications among English Wikipedia editors and students. We find that these identifications draw on a diversity of evidentiary types, from discrete textual features and judgments about content to circular sense-making claims and presumptions about the author's moral character, and that they are contradictory, dynamic, and contextually dependent. In practice, what often motivates an AI identification is not the presence of any specific textual element, but pragmatic infelicity: a perceived mismatch between the elements present and those deemed appropriate within the text's particular context. Consequently, real-world identifications of AI-generated writing do not merely neutrally point out the perceived presence of "AI-like" features, but can be leveraged as a discursive resource to maintain and define sociolinguistic norms: what can be said in a given context, what is appropriate, and, ultimately, what is human. Further, the design and implementation of systems and tools that treat AI-likeness as a context-free property of text may therefore inherit, and enforce at scale, the norms of whichever contexts they were trained on.</p></details>
